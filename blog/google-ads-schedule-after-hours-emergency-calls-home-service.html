Should Your Google Ads Run at Night? Ad Scheduling for After-Hours and Emergency Calls

Published November 1, 2026 · 5 min read

For a lot of home service work, the phone rings at the worst possible hour: a burst pipe at 10 p.m., no heat on a Sunday, a tripping panel on a holiday. Google Ads lets you decide which hours your ads show, which means "should I run at night?" is a real decision, not a default. The right answer depends on one question: who picks up the phone, and what happens if nobody does?

What Ad Scheduling Actually Does

Ad scheduling lets you choose the days and hours your ads are eligible to run, and raise or lower your bids for specific windows. By default an account shows ads around the clock. You can turn off the hours you don't want, or keep them on and bid more or less aggressively at certain times. It's a control over when you spend, not a way to get more clicks.

Three Common Setups

  • Business hours only. Ads run when you or your staff can answer. This is the safest option for a one-truck shop where the owner is also the dispatcher and doesn't want calls at midnight.
  • Extended hours. Ads run early mornings and evenings, when people are home and noticing problems, even if the office is closed. Calls can go to a phone you answer or to a form you reply to first thing.
  • True 24/7 emergency. Ads run all day and night because you actually offer emergency service and someone answers. This only works if the promise is real, since a caller with a flooded basement will call the next company after a couple of unanswered rings.
The test to apply Don't ask "are there searches at night?" Ask "if a night click turns into a call, will a person answer, and will I be glad they did?" If the answer is no, you're paying for clicks that can't become jobs.

Check Your Own Data First

Google Ads can break your results down by hour of day and day of week. Look at your account over a few weeks and compare two things for each time window: how much you spent and how many calls or form leads came in. Don't judge on a handful of clicks, because small numbers swing wildly. If you have call tracking, check whether the night calls were real jobs or wrong numbers and price shoppers. If you don't, how to tell which calls your Google Ads are actually producing explains how to set it up.

Your Trade Changes the Answer

Urgency varies. Plumbing and HVAC often have genuine emergencies, where someone searching at night wants a person now. Electrical can too. Roofing and landscaping are usually planned purchases, so a late-night click is more likely someone browsing for a quote tomorrow, which suggests a form they can fill out and a prompt reply in the morning. Treat that as a starting assumption and let your own numbers overrule it.

Make After-Hours Calls Worth Paying For

  • Say it on the ad and the site. If you offer emergency service, state it clearly. If you don't, don't imply it.
  • Have a plan for the phone. Calls can ring your cell, a partner's, or a service that answers. Whatever you pick, a real voicemail greeting that promises a callback time beats a full mailbox.
  • Give nights a second option. A short contact form, as covered in what to put on your website contact form, lets people reach you without a call.
  • Reply fast in the morning. A form lead from 11 p.m. that gets answered at 7:30 a.m. is far more likely to book than one that waits until afternoon.

Watch the Budget Side

Evening and weekend clicks come out of the same daily budget as everything else. If night traffic eats the budget before your best daytime hours, you can lose profitable calls. Scheduling helps here, as does the seasonal planning in how to plan your Google Ads budget around the busy and slow seasons. And if you're often unreachable during the day, what to do when you miss calls from Google Ads is the place to start.
