How to Read Your Google Ads Search Terms Report (and Stop Paying for Junk Clicks)

Published October 25, 2026 · 5 min read

Most owners look at clicks and cost in their Google Ads account and stop there. The most revealing page is one level deeper: the search terms report. It lists the actual words people typed before they clicked your ad. If you've ever wondered where your budget went, this is the page that answers it.

Keywords vs. Search Terms

A keyword is what you tell Google you want to show up for, like "water heater repair." A search term is what a real person typed, like "water heater repair" or "how to fix a water heater pilot light myself." Google can match your keywords to searches that are close but not identical, so the two lists don't always line up. The search terms report is where you see the difference.

Where to Find It

In your Google Ads account, open the Insights and reports section (or Keywords, depending on the version you see) and look for "Search terms." Set the date range to the last 30 days or longer, and sort by cost or clicks so the biggest spenders are on top. You're looking for two things: searches that cost you money without ever becoming a call, and searches that bring real customers that you should lean into.

The Kinds of Junk Clicks Home Service Owners Usually Find

  • Do-it-yourself searches. "How to unclog a drain," "how to replace an outlet." These people want instructions, not a pro.
  • Job seekers. "Plumber jobs near me," "HVAC technician salary," "electrician apprenticeship." They click, but they're looking for work, not hiring.
  • Wrong service. A roofer who doesn't do gutters showing up for "gutter cleaning," or an HVAC company that only does residential showing up for commercial refrigeration.
  • Wrong place. Searches naming a town or state you don't serve.
  • Parts and products. "Buy water heater element," "furnace filter size chart." Shoppers, not customers.
  • Free or cheapest-possible. "Free estimate" is fine for many trades, but "free" next to words like "repair" or "cheap" can pull in people who will never book.
A simple test for each search term Ask: would I want this person to call me right now? If the answer is clearly no, it's a candidate to block. If you're unsure, leave it and watch whether calls come from it.

What to Do With What You Find

The tool for blocking junk is negative keywords. You add a word or phrase to a negative keyword list, and your ads stop showing for searches that include it. Common starting points for home service accounts are words like "jobs," "salary," "training," "course," "diy," and "how to," plus the names of services you don't offer. Be careful, though. Block too broadly and you can cut off good searches. For example, blocking "free" might also block "free estimate," which a lot of customers search on purpose. Add terms one at a time, and think about whether a real customer could ever type that word.

Don't Only Look for What's Bad

The report also shows you what's working. If you see searches like "emergency plumber open now" or "AC not cooling same day" turning into calls, that tells you what your customers actually say. Those phrases belong in your ad text and on your landing page. A search term that keeps bringing calls but isn't a keyword you set up can be added as one, so you control it instead of relying on Google's matching.

Match Types Change What You'll See

Broad match lets Google show your ad for a wide range of related searches, so the search terms report tends to be messier. Phrase and exact match are tighter and give you more control. Neither is automatically right. Broad match with strong negative keywords and good conversion tracking can work, and tight match can limit volume. What matters is that someone is checking the report and adjusting. Tracking which searches lead to real calls is covered in how to tell which calls your Google Ads are producing.

How Often to Check

In the first few weeks of a new campaign, look at it every few days, because that's when the most waste shows up. After that, a weekly look is usually enough, and a monthly review is the minimum. A campaign nobody reviews slowly fills up with searches that never should have been paid for.
