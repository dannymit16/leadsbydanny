How Much Does Google Ads Cost for Landscaping Companies?

Published September 29, 2026 · 5 min read

Landscaping is one of the few trades where a single customer can be worth $60 or $60,000 depending on what they hire you for. A one-time mulch job, a weekly mowing contract that runs for years, and a full backyard design-build all start with someone typing "landscaper near me." That's why "what should I spend on Google Ads?" doesn't have one answer for a landscaping company — it depends on which of those customers you're actually trying to buy.

Decide Which Customer You're Paying For

Before picking a budget, pick a target. The three most common landscaping lead types behave very differently in an ad account:

  • Recurring maintenance: Lower ticket up front, but a customer who stays for seasons is worth far more than the first invoice suggests. Worth paying more per lead for, if you can actually keep the route dense.
  • Design-build and hardscape: Patios, retaining walls, outdoor kitchens. Fewer searches, longer decisions, and the highest value per job. These leads are usually worth a much higher cost per lead than a routine service call.
  • One-off cleanups and seasonal jobs: Spring cleanup, leaf removal, sod. High volume, price-shopped, and easy to overspend on if they share a budget with everything else.

If all three share a single budget and a single campaign, Google tends to chase whichever generates the most clicks — which is usually the cheapest, least valuable one.

How Budgets Actually Break Down

Like any trade, there are two separate costs, and quotes that blur them together are hard to compare.

  • Ad spend (paid to Google): This is the money that buys clicks. A small crew mostly after maintenance routes can learn a lot from a modest monthly budget; a design-build company competing for high-value projects in a larger market will need more to stay visible on the searches that matter. How much depends on your market, your service area, and how many new jobs you can realistically take on.
  • Management fee (paid to whoever runs the campaign): Flat monthly fees and percentage-of-spend fees are both common. Ask which one you're being quoted, and what it actually includes, before comparing two numbers that may not measure the same thing.
The number that matters Cost per click is the easiest number for anyone to quote and the least useful. What you want to know is cost per lead, and then what a lead is worth to you — a maintenance customer who stays for three seasons changes that math completely compared to a one-time job.

Seasons Change the Math

Landscaping demand is not flat, and neither should your spend be. Spring cleanup and planting searches build fast as the weather warms, fall brings leaf removal and aeration, and in colder regions winter is close to dead for most services. A campaign that runs the same budget all year either overspends in the slow months or leaves money on the table when demand peaks. Planning the budget around your real calendar — and keeping maintenance-contract offers running in the shoulder months when people are thinking ahead — usually beats a flat monthly number.

Keep Your Service Area Tight

Landscaping is a route-based business. A lead 40 minutes outside your normal route costs you drive time on every visit, even if the first job pays well. Limiting your campaign to the neighborhoods and zip codes you actually want to serve keeps ad spend on customers you can profitably reach — and stops you from paying for clicks you'd end up turning down.

Where the Website Fits In

Ad money only pays off if the page the click lands on makes it easy to act. For landscapers, that usually means clear service categories, real project photos, and a fast, obvious way to request a quote from a phone. A great campaign pointed at a slow or confusing page is one of the most common reasons ad spend feels wasted.
