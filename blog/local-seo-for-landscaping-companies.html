Local SEO for Landscaping Companies: Where Photos and Seasons Do the Heavy Lifting

Published September 25, 2026 · 6 min read

Landscaping has a split personality that most local SEO advice doesn't account for. Half your search traffic is someone who wants their lawn mowed every other week for the rest of the season — low drama, recurring, price-sensitive. The other half is someone typing "landscape design near me" after deciding to redo their backyard, comparing photos and willing to spend real money on the right company. Ranking for both means treating them differently instead of running one generic strategy at everything.

Set Up Categories for Maintenance and Design Separately

Google Business Profile lets you pick multiple categories, and landscaping companies that only choose "Landscaper" are leaving visibility on the table. If you do mowing and maintenance, add "Lawn care service." If you do design and installs, add "Landscape designer." If hardscape, irrigation, or tree work are real parts of the business, add those too — "Landscaping contractor," "Sprinkler system contractor," "Tree service," whichever actually apply. Each category is a separate door Google can walk a searcher through, and a profile set up as just "Landscaper" misses searches phrased more specifically than that.

Photos Do More Work Here Than in Almost Any Other Trade

This is the biggest difference between landscaping and a trade like HVAC or plumbing: the work is visible, and people are actively shopping on what they can see. A Google Business Profile with a handful of stock-looking photos or none at all loses to a competitor with real, recent job photos — a fresh mow, a finished paver patio, a before-and-after of a backyard redesign. Upload photos regularly, not just once when the profile is created, and keep a simple habit of snapping a few shots after every install or bigger maintenance job. This is one of the lowest-effort, highest-payoff things a landscaping company can do for local search, and most competitors still aren't doing it consistently.

Match Your Content to the Actual Calendar

Search demand for landscaping swings hard with the seasons, and it swings differently depending on what you offer. Spring cleanup and mulch searches spike in March and April. Mowing and maintenance searches run steady through summer. Fall cleanup and leaf removal pick up in October and November. If you're in a region that gets snow, plowing searches take over in winter. A website or Google Business Profile that says the same thing in January as it does in June is missing the moment. A short seasonal post or Google update — "now scheduling spring cleanups," "fall leaf removal booking" — costs almost nothing to write and matches exactly what people are searching for that month.

Time Reviews Differently for Maintenance vs. Install Customers

A recurring maintenance customer isn't going to leave a review after every mow — asking too often just gets ignored. The better moment is a few weeks into the season, once they've noticed the yard consistently looks good and the crew shows up reliably. A design or install customer is the opposite: ask shortly after the job is finished, while the new patio or landscaping is still fresh and they're showing it off to neighbors. Sending the same review request script to both groups at the same interval wastes the easiest reviews you could be getting.

NAP Consistency and Proof of Insurance

Most states don't license landscapers the way they license plumbers or electricians, so licensing isn't usually the trust signal to lean on. Insurance is. Being clear that you're insured, and consistent about your business name, address, and phone number across your website, Google Business Profile, Yelp, Angi, and Nextdoor, matters more here than people expect — Nextdoor in particular carries real weight for landscaping since it's where a lot of neighborhood recommendations actually happen.

Service-Area Pages: Separate Maintenance Intent From Design Intent

If you cover multiple towns, a page per town can help — but a single generic "landscaping services in [town]" page that lists everything from mowing to hardscape tends to underperform two focused pages. Someone searching "lawn mowing service [town]" wants pricing and scheduling; someone searching "landscape design [town]" wants a portfolio and a sense of style. Splitting those out, with real photos on each, does more for both rankings and conversions than one page trying to be everything.

What's a waste of money in 2026 Paying for a dozen thin, near-identical service-area pages with no real photos on them, buying reviews, or an "SEO package" that never once mentions uploading photos to your Google Business Profile. For a visual trade like this, an agency that isn't talking about your photo habit isn't looking at what actually moves the needle.

What This Actually Costs You

The category setup, the photo habit, the seasonal posts, the review timing — all of that is time, not money, and most of it is a five-minute habit once it's routine. Well-built service-area pages with a real photo gallery take longer to put together and are a fair thing to pay someone for if you'd rather spend that time on job sites instead.

How Long Before You See Anything

A well-categorized profile with fresh, real photos can start showing improvement in the local map pack within a few weeks, especially during a season when search volume is already climbing. Seasonal content compounds — the fall cleanup post you write this year helps again next October. Nobody can promise you a ranking or a date; anyone who does is guessing. What's in your control is keeping the photo habit and the seasonal timing consistent instead of only thinking about your online presence when the phone goes quiet.
