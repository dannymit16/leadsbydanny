Should Google Ads Send Clicks to Your Homepage or a Dedicated Landing Page?

Published October 26, 2026 · 4 min read

When you pay for a click, you choose where that person lands. A lot of home service owners never make that choice on purpose. The ad points at the homepage because that's the page that already exists. It's one of the quietest ways to lose calls, because the ads look like they're working while the page does half the job.

Why the Homepage Is a Weak Landing Spot

A homepage has to serve everyone: past customers checking your hours, someone comparing you to a competitor, a job applicant, a person who heard your name from a neighbor. So it talks about everything. Someone who just searched "water heater not heating" doesn't want a tour. They want to know within a few seconds that you fix that, that you serve their area, and how to reach you. If they have to scroll and hunt for it, many will tap back and try the next ad.

What a Dedicated Landing Page Does Differently

A landing page has one job: get the person who clicked a specific ad to call or send a request. It repeats what they searched, answers the obvious questions, and removes distractions like a big menu with ten links to other pages. The page matches the ad, and the ad matches the search.

  • A headline that echoes the search. "Furnace repair in your area" beats a generic "Welcome to our company."
  • A tap-to-call button that's visible without scrolling on a phone. Most home service searches happen on phones, often in a hurry.
  • Proof near the top. Your license and insurance if you hold them, real reviews, and real photos of your work.
  • The service area stated plainly, so people outside it don't waste a call and you don't waste a click.
  • One simple form as the backup. Name, phone, what's going on. Not ten fields.
  • Few exits. No link trail that sends people wandering off before they've contacted you.
A quick test Search for your own service on your phone, tap your own ad, and give yourself five seconds. Can you tell what they do, where they work, and how to call? If not, a stranger with a leaking pipe won't get further than you did.

One Page or Several?

You don't need a landing page for every possible search. Start with the split that matches how your customers think. An HVAC company's "no heat tonight" caller and "quote on a new system" shopper are in different moods and need different pages. A plumber's emergency leak and a planned repipe are the same story. A landscaper's weekly maintenance lead and a patio design lead are not the same customer. If two kinds of searches need different answers, they deserve different pages. If they'd read the same page the same way, one is fine.

When the Homepage Is Okay

If your homepage is already simple, focused on one or two services, loads fast on a phone, and puts the phone number and proof up top, it can work as a landing page for a small, general campaign. The problem isn't the homepage as such. It's a homepage built to impress everyone that is being asked to convert someone in a hurry. If you're not sure, what a site needs once it's carrying ad traffic is a useful place to compare against.

How to Tell Whether It's Working

Compare the calls and requests coming from ad clicks to the clicks themselves. If you pay for plenty of clicks and the phone stays quiet, the page is the first thing to look at, before blaming the keywords. That only works if calls are tracked, which is covered in how to tell which calls your Google Ads are producing. It also pays to check the search terms report, because the right page for the wrong searches still won't convert.

Speed matters too. A page that takes several seconds to load on a cell connection loses people before they read a word, and image-heavy pages are a common cause.
