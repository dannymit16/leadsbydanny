What to Write in Your Google Ads Headlines: Ad Copy That Gets Home Service Calls

Published November 2, 2026 · 5 min read

Most home service owners spend a lot of time on their budget and keywords and almost none on the ad itself. But the ad is the last thing a person reads before deciding whether to call you or the next result. Good ad copy is not clever. It is specific, it matches what the person just typed, and it makes the next step obvious.

How Google Ads Text Works Now

Most search campaigns use responsive search ads. Instead of writing one fixed ad, you supply a pool of headlines (up to 15) and descriptions (up to 4), and Google mixes them into combinations to show. That means every headline has to make sense next to almost any other headline, and you can't count on a specific line appearing in a specific spot unless you pin it.

Match the Search First

Someone typing "water heater not working" and someone typing "tankless water heater installation" want different things. If both see the same generic "Quality Plumbing Services" ad, neither feels spoken to. Group your campaigns so each ad group covers one kind of job, then write headlines that name that job in the words people actually use. Repair, replacement, installation, and emergency work should not share a single ad if you can help it.

A simple headline checklist Across your 15 headlines, make sure you have the service, the city or area you cover, a reason to call you, and a clear action. If a headline does none of those, it is probably filler.

What Belongs in the Headlines

  • The service itself. "Furnace Repair," "Panel Upgrades," "Roof Leak Repair," "Lawn Maintenance." Plain words beat brand slogans.
  • Where you work. Your city or the area you serve. It tells the right people they're in the right place and quietly filters out the wrong ones. Pair this with the service-area settings in how to set your Google Ads service area.
  • Reasons to choose you, only if true. Licensed and insured, family-owned, same-day appointments, free estimates, emergency service. Only claim what you actually offer and can back up on the phone.
  • An action. "Call Today," "Request a Quote," "Book Online." Tell people what to do next.

What to Put in the Descriptions

Descriptions are where you can add a sentence of detail: what's covered, how quickly you respond, how people can reach you. Keep them concrete. "Licensed electricians for panel upgrades and EV charger installs. Call for a quote" tells someone more than "We provide the best service in town." If you offer emergency work, say it. If you don't, don't hint at it, because that sets up a missed expectation, and a missed call costs you the click you paid for.

Filler That Wastes Space

  • "Best" and "#1" with nothing to support them.
  • Your company name in five different headlines.
  • Vague lines like "Quality Service You Can Trust" that every competitor also uses.
  • Offers you don't actually honor, such as a discount that disappears on the phone.
  • Claims you can't prove, like response times you don't reliably meet.

Assets That Earn Their Place

Beyond headlines and descriptions, Google lets you attach extra pieces to your ads. For home service, a few are worth the effort:

  • Call asset. Adds your phone number so mobile users can tap to call directly from the ad. Make sure the number on the ad is the one you track, as covered in how to tell which calls your Google Ads are producing.
  • Location asset. Connects your Google Business Profile so your address or service area can appear with the ad.
  • Sitelinks. Extra links to specific pages, such as a service page or a quote form. They only help if those pages exist and are good.
  • Callouts. Short phrases like "Licensed & Insured" or "Free Estimates," again only if true.

Make the Click Land Somewhere That Matches

Strong ad copy sets an expectation. If the ad says "Water Heater Replacement" and the click lands on a generic homepage, you've broken the promise. Whether to send ads to your homepage or a landing page covers how to keep the message consistent from search to page.

Test, But Slowly

Google reports how each headline and description performs, labeling some as stronger or weaker. With a small account there often isn't enough data to draw firm conclusions from a few clicks, so change a little at a time and give each change a few weeks. Look at calls and booked jobs, not just click rates, since a catchy ad that attracts the wrong people is worse than a plain one that attracts the right ones.
