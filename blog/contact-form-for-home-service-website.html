What to Put on Your Website Contact Form (and What to Leave Off)

Published October 29, 2026 · 5 min read

Plenty of visitors would rather type than call, especially at night or when they're at work. They find your site, tap "Request a Quote," and meet a form. If that form asks for too much, or promises nothing about what happens next, many of them close the page and try the next company. Here's how I think about what belongs on it.

Start With the Minimum You Need to Call Back

The goal of the form isn't to collect a full job file. It's to get a conversation started. For most home service companies the real minimum is:

  • Name. One field is enough. Splitting first and last name adds a box for no reason.
  • Phone number. This is the field you'll actually use. Most owners call back faster than they email.
  • What they need. A short dropdown or a single open text box, such as "What's going on?"

Everything beyond that has to earn its place. Each extra field is another small reason to stop.

Fields That Usually Cost You More Than They Give

  • Full street address up front. It feels like a big ask from a stranger. A ZIP code is usually enough to tell whether they're in your service area, and you can get the address on the phone.
  • Required email. If you plan to call, don't force it. Make it optional or drop it.
  • Long "how did you hear about us" menus. Useful to you, irrelevant to them. If you want it, make it optional and last.
  • Budget questions. Asking for a budget before you've talked often reads as a filter, and a lot of people don't know what the job costs.
  • Heavy spam puzzles. Some captcha setups frustrate real customers on phones. A hidden honeypot field blocks most junk without making anyone click traffic lights.

Where a Few Extra Fields Do Help

Some trades genuinely benefit from one or two more answers, as long as they're easy:

  • Job type. A dropdown that matches your service pages (for example, repair vs. replacement) saves you a question on the callback.
  • Urgency. A simple "Is this an emergency?" choice lets you jump the right requests to the front of your list, and tells you who needs a call tonight and who is planning a project.
  • A photo upload. For roofing, landscaping, or panel work, a picture can save a trip. Keep it optional, since uploading is slow on a weak signal.
A quick test Open your own form on your phone with one thumb and fill it in as a stranger would. If you hit anything that makes you hesitate, tiny tap targets, the wrong keyboard for the phone field, a required field you don't understand, your customers hit it too.

Make It Work on a Phone

Most visits to a home service site happen on a phone, so the details matter. The phone field should bring up the number keypad. Labels should stay visible, not vanish when someone starts typing. The submit button should be easy to hit and say what it does, such as "Request My Quote," rather than a bare "Submit." And a tap-to-call button should sit right beside the form, because some people will look at the form and decide to call instead. That's a good outcome, not a failure.

Say What Happens Next

A form that just says "Thanks!" leaves people wondering. A confirmation that says who will call, from what kind of number, and roughly when, only promise what you can actually do, builds trust and cuts down on duplicate submissions. If you only answer during business hours, say so. Telling someone "we'll call you within one business day" and then doing it beats a vague promise of "right away."

The Part Most Owners Skip

The best form in the world fails if nobody reads the notifications. Check where submissions are sent, whether they reach a phone you actually carry, and whether they land in spam. Then submit a test every few months. A lead that sits in an unchecked inbox for two days has often already hired someone else, especially if it came from a paid click.
