How to Tell Which Calls Your Google Ads Are Actually Producing

Published October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

Most owners who run Google Ads eventually hit the same wall: the dashboard says one thing, the phone says another. The report shows 40 "conversions" and you remember maybe 12 real conversations. Or the opposite — the report looks thin, but the schedule is full and you can't tell whether the ads had anything to do with it. Both problems come from the same place: nobody set up a clean way to connect a phone ring to the ad that caused it.

Why "Conversions" Can Lie to You

A conversion is only whatever the account was told to count. If it counts every tap on the phone number, that includes someone who tapped by accident, someone who hung up in two seconds, and your own employee testing the site. If it counts every page view of a "thank you" page, a single person refreshing the page counts twice. Neither number is dishonest, exactly — it's just not the number you care about, which is how many real jobs did this turn into.

The Four Places a Lead Can Come From

  • Calls from the ad itself. On a phone, Google can show a call button right in the ad. Those calls can be reported without ever touching your website.
  • Taps on the phone number on your site. Someone clicks the ad, lands on your page, and taps your number. This only gets counted if the site is set up to report it.
  • Calls from people who dial by hand. They saw your site on a laptop, then picked up their phone and typed the number. This is the group most often missed, and it can be a big share of the calls in trades where the job is a major purchase, like roofing or a system replacement.
  • Form submissions. Quote requests and estimate forms. These are the easiest to count and the easiest to over-count if spam gets through.
The simplest honest test For two weeks, have whoever answers the phone ask one question on every new call: "How did you find us?" Write the answer down. It takes ten seconds, costs nothing, and gives you a number to compare against what the dashboard claims.

What to Ask for in a Call Report

If someone manages your ads, you should be able to see more than a total. Ask for these, in plain English:

  • How many calls came in, and how many lasted long enough to be a real conversation?
  • Which search terms and which ads led to the calls?
  • How many calls were missed, and at what times of day?
  • How many of the calls turned into booked estimates or jobs, according to your own records?

That last one is the part no ad platform can do for you. Only you know which callers became customers, so closing that loop — even with a simple list of "booked / didn't book" next to each lead — is what turns a vague report into a real cost per job.

Missed Calls Are Part of the Tracking Problem

A lot of what looks like "bad leads" is really missed leads. If your ads run during hours nobody is answering, or calls roll to a voicemail that nobody checks until the next morning, the person has usually already called the next company on the list. Looking at call times next to your ad schedule often shows the fix isn't a different keyword — it's making sure the phone is answered, or the ads are paused, when you can't take the call.

Mistakes That Make the Data Useless

  • Counting everything as equal. A 4-second call and a 6-minute call shouldn't count the same.
  • Never checking the spam. Form leads from bots or people selling services look like conversions until someone reads them.
  • Judging by week, not by month. Calls for bigger jobs are lumpy; a single week can look great or terrible for no reason.
  • Not knowing who owns the account. If the tracking lives in someone else's account, you lose the history the day you leave. Your ad account should be yours.
