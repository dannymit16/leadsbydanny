Photos for Your Home Service Website: Real Job Photos vs. Stock Images

Published November 3, 2026 · 5 min read

When someone lands on a home service website, they're deciding in a few seconds whether a real company is behind it. Photos do a lot of that work. A page full of smiling models in spotless uniforms says one thing; a truck with your name on it and a finished job says another. Here's how I think about photos for a contractor site.

Why Stock Photos Hurt More Than They Help

Most homeowners have seen the same handshake, the same hard hat on a blueprint, the same perfect kitchen. They may not consciously notice, but the page feels generic. For a business that sells trust, that's the wrong feeling. Stock images also say nothing about what you actually do, where you work, or how well you do it.

Stock isn't banned. A simple background or an icon-style graphic is fine. But the photos that sit next to your service descriptions and your phone number should be yours.

The Photos Worth Having

  • You and your crew. One honest photo of the people who will show up at the door. It doesn't need to be polished. It needs to be you.
  • Your truck or van. It proves you're a real, local, marked-vehicle business.
  • Finished work. A cleaned-up install, a new roof line, a tidy panel, a freshly edged lawn. This is the proof of what you sell.
  • Before and after pairs. Strong for roofing, landscaping, and repairs where the change is visible. Less useful where the work ends up behind a wall.
  • Work in progress. Showing the process, such as a properly supported pipe run or a neat wiring job, shows care that a finished surface hides.

Taking Usable Photos With Your Phone

You don't need a photographer. A current phone is enough if you follow a few habits:

  • Wipe the lens. It's the most common reason for a hazy shot.
  • Shoot in daylight when you can, and avoid pointing straight into the sun.
  • Turn the phone sideways. Landscape photos fit a website far better than tall ones.
  • Step back so the whole job is in frame, then take a second close-up of the detail.
  • Clear tools, trash, and cones out of the shot first. Thirty seconds of tidying changes the picture.
Build the habit, not the project Don't plan a big photo day. Take two or three pictures at the end of every job as part of closing it out. After a few months you'll have far more than the site needs, and you can pick the best.

Respect Your Customers

Photograph the work, not the people or the house number. Avoid faces, license plates, mail, and anything that identifies the home or shows valuables. When a job is particularly personal or recognizable, ask first. It costs nothing and keeps goodwill.

Keep Photos From Slowing the Site Down

Straight from a phone, a single photo can be several megabytes. Put ten on a page and it crawls on a weak signal, which is exactly where many of your visitors are. Photos should be resized and compressed before they go on the site, and only the first screen of images should load immediately. Slow pages lose people before the photos can do any good, and fast, well-sized images still look sharp on a phone.

Add Plain Descriptions

Give each photo a short caption or alt text that says what it is and roughly where: "Tankless water heater install" or "Retaining wall finished this fall." That helps visitors who can't see the image, and it gives search engines a clearer picture of what the page covers. Skip stuffing it with keywords. Describe it the way you'd describe it to a person.
