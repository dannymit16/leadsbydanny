Missing Calls While You're on the Job? How to Stop Losing Google Ads Leads

Published October 23, 2026 · 4 min read

You're under a sink, in an attic, or on a roof when the phone buzzes in your pocket. You can't answer it. The call came from a Google Ad you're paying for, and the person on the other end is probably already calling the next company on the list. For a one-truck or small-crew business, this is one of the most common ways ad money gets wasted, and it has nothing to do with the ad itself.

Why a Missed Call Costs More Than It Looks

Someone searching for a plumber, an AC repair, or an electrician is usually comparing a few companies at once. They call, and if nobody picks up, they move on to the next number. They rarely leave a long message and wait. You paid for the click, and the job went to whoever answered. That is a different problem from a bad website or bad ads, and no amount of ad tweaking fixes it.

First, Find Out How Many Calls You're Missing

Before changing anything, look at your actual phone log for a week or two. Count the calls from unknown numbers that went unanswered and note the time of day. Most owners find a pattern: mornings when they're on the first job, or the middle of a long install. If you're using call tracking, it will show you which missed calls came from ads. If you aren't, our post on how to track calls from Google Ads covers the basics.

The question to ask Of the calls I missed last week, how many did I actually call back, and how fast? If the answer is "some, eventually," that's the gap to close first.

Practical Fixes, From Cheapest to Most Involved

  • A voicemail greeting that does its job. Say your company name, say you're on a job, and ask for their name, address, and what's going on. Tell them roughly when they'll hear back. A caller who knows what happens next is more likely to wait.
  • Call back fast, and in a batch. Set a habit: between jobs, or at a set time like lunch, return every missed call. Calling back within minutes beats calling back that evening, because the caller may not have hired anyone yet.
  • An automatic text after a missed call. Many business phone services can send a short text like "Sorry we missed you, we're on a job, can we call you back at this number?" Check that your phone provider supports this before counting on it, and keep the message short and honest.
  • Someone else answering. A spouse, an office person, or an answering service can take the first call and pass details to you. This costs money, so it makes more sense once missed calls are clearly costing you jobs.
  • A form as a second option. Not everyone wants to leave voicemail. A simple contact form on your site gives people another way to reach you when the phone goes unanswered.

Use Ad Settings to Match When You Can Answer

Google Ads lets you control when your ads show and when the call button appears. If you reliably can't answer during certain hours, you can adjust the ad schedule, or limit calling to the hours you or your staff are actually reachable, while the website form catches the rest. This is the same logic as trimming ads when you're booked out, which we cover in should you pause Google Ads when you're booked out. The goal isn't to show fewer ads for its own sake. It's to stop paying for calls that have no realistic chance of being answered.

It Depends on Your Trade

Emergency trades like plumbing, HVAC, and electrical lose the most from a missed call, because the customer needs help now and won't wait for a callback tomorrow. For roofing and landscaping, a quick callback within a few hours is usually still in time, since the customer is often collecting estimates over several days. Be realistic about which category most of your calls fall into when deciding how much to spend on fixing this.

Don't Blame the Ads Too Fast

If the ads are producing calls and the calls aren't turning into jobs, check the answer rate before deciding the campaign doesn't work. A campaign that sends ten calls where you answer four is a different problem from a campaign that sends four calls. Fixing the first one usually costs far less than buying more clicks.
